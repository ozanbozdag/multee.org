Outreach / Interactive model

Grow a snowflake yeast cluster

A snowflake yeast cluster reproduces by breaking. Cells stay attached after division, the growing branches crowd against one another, and strain builds on the bonds between mother and daughter cells until one snaps, releasing a branch that grows on as a cluster of its own.

Interactive · Uncalibrated model, dimensionless units

This section carries an interactive 3D simulator, which needs JavaScript and a recent browser. It grows a cluster from one cell: each cell buds, its daughters stay attached, contacts between cells push the branches apart, and a bond breaks when it is stretched past a set threshold, leaving two clusters that both keep growing.

Things to try

Ask the model a question

Try changing a variable to investigate its effect on the properties of the cluster.

  • Watch for the first break. Press Run with the default settings. Once a bond snaps, a white outline marks the cluster that still holds the founding cell and coloured outlines mark the pieces that broke away, each of which keeps growing and can break again.
  • Compare bond strengths. Run the three presets in turn. They share a seed and differ only in bond strength, so any difference in when the first branch breaks off, and in how many clusters you end up with, comes from that one setting.
  • Change the shape of the cells. In the MuLTEE's anaerobic lines, cells evolved to be more elongate, lowering packing density and delaying fracture (The Story). Move the shape slider, apply the change, and ask what cell shape alone does to a growing body when nothing else changes.
  • Change the seed. The seed fixes the random sequence of bud positions and budding times, so the same settings can grow a visibly different body, showing how much of the outcome rides on chance events at each bud. Do multiple runs with the same conditions but different seeds for an exercise in investigating the importance of repeats!

The model

What it includes, and what it leaves out

This is a deliberately simple model. Cells are rigid ellipsoids that grow, bud and push against their neighbours. Each bond between a mother and daughter stretches like a spring, bends a little (yielding permanently past a modest angle), and breaks for good once it is stretched past the threshold you set. Nothing tells a cluster when or where to split: every break comes out of the mechanics of growth and crowding. All units are dimensionless and nothing is calibrated to measured yeast, so the simulator shows how a branched body can generate its own reproduction, not how large or how fast a real cluster grows.

The model also counts every detached piece as a new cluster, without asking whether that piece could actually escape its neighbours. Branch entanglement, which let evolved MuLTEE clusters hold together after many mother-daughter bonds had broken, is therefore outside its scope.

Model details and numerical limits

Oriented, rigid ellipsoids grow and bud with elastic surface attachments and modest angular plasticity. A mature cell has size 1 and a newborn starts at 0.23; changing the shape preserves cell volume. Every cell places its first successful daughter at its distal pole, along the positive long axis. Later daughters use the selected 0–90° initial growth-axis angle. Their attachment sites are independent of this direction: a fixed surface ring at 90% of the mother's half-length toward its distal pole, with random azimuth. The newborn's basal pole starts 0.25 model units from its attachment site along its own axis. Contacts and elastic bending can change the axes after birth; the selected angle is an initial condition, not a permanent angular constraint. Each newborn begins its own reproductive history.

Repulsion and torque from cell contacts rearrange branches. Contact uses orientation-dependent ellipsoid overlap classification (Perram–Wertheim) with an approximate soft-penalty depth and normal. Bending below 12° from the current preferred angle is elastic. Beyond 12°, the preferred angle gradually changes; that retained change is plastic. Each bond has a cumulative 25° plastic-rotation allowance. Axial rest length does not change. Failure uses axial tensile strain only, is irreversible, and is checked during numerical relaxation rather than after a fully equilibrated fracture solve. More than one bond can fail in a substep. Connected components define clusters. Clusters keep growing and contacting each other in the mechanical model; no cuts or separating kicks are imposed on the solver.

Mechanics are overdamped, with illustrative spring, bending and contact parameters. Growth advances only when overlap is at most 0.035 and residual motion is at most 0.0015 after relaxation, so some updates relax the same cells without advancing their growth tick, and growth can appear uneven. The solver compares one full step with two half steps to estimate numerical error and accepts only the two-half-step path. A persistent stall means this numerical settling criterion remains unresolved, not that biological jamming has been established. A solver fault retains the last finite state and is not a biological outcome.

Growth schedules, mobility, neck geometry, stiffness and time step are illustrative. These are not calibrated forces, physical times or predictions of yeast phenotypes. The cluster separation and jiggle are rigid display transforms of whole clusters; they never alter the forces, strain or fracture calculation.
